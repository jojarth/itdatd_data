The basic rules of play are simple. One Player takes the roll of the Game Master ("GM"), who acts as part referee, part storyteller. All other players, usually between 3-5 people, take the roll of Player Characters ("PC" or "Character"). The GM will describe an environment your character is in, adversaries they are up against, or situations your character finds themselves in, and you tell the GM what your character wishes to do. The GM will then describe what happens or may leave the outcome to a dice roll.

The worlds that ITD&TD present should be very dangerous, and it is highly recommended that when you create your Character, you create a character who is willing to adventure, and willing to be part of a group of allies, often called a "Party". Characters thinking of themselves as "lone wolves" or "not needing help" don't last long in this dangerous world.

## Basic Rules
After describing what you wish your character would do, the GM will narrate the results. Often times, the outcome of this narration may be determined by a dice roll. There are several types of dice rolls that may need to be made in ITD&TD, and they can be used in a variety of situations. The most common types of dice roll mechanics are ***Attack Rolls, Damage\Healing Rolls, Saving Throws, Attribute Tests***, and ***x-in-y check***.

### Attack Rolls
Attack Rolls are made when you are attempting to strike a creature with a weapon, or an unarmed strike. Attack rolls might also be made as part of wrestling attempts, archery competitions, or even attempts to do tricky combat maneuvers. Attack rolls will use your character classes Combat Bonus.

When making an attack roll, you will roll a d20, add the relevant modifiers, and compare the result to the Armor Class of the target. If your total roll is equal to or higher than the target's Armor Class, you succeed, and can deal damage with your weapon.

Attack Rolls are gone over in more detail in Chapter 08 - Combat.

<GameplayCard
  color="green"
  title="Attack Roll Example"
>
You are a level 1 fighter, you have a Brawn score of 16, which gives you a +0 to attack rolls with most weapons, and a Combat Bonus of +2. To make an attack roll, you roll a d20 and add 2. If the target of your attack had an AC of 12, you would need to roll at least a 10 on the d20 (10 + 2 = 12) to succeed at dealing damage to the target.
</GameplayCard>

### Damage\Healing Rolls
Damage Rolls are used to determine the damage a creature takes from a weapon, spell, or environmental effect. Damage rolls are usually dice rolls, with the type of dice stated by the effect that causes damage. Many damage rolls also have a static number component, which is added or subtracted from the dice result. Damage rolls with weapons that your character is Familiar or Cross-Trained with apply your Combat Bonus to damage.

Healing Rolls are used to determine how much healing a creature receives from a spell or effect. Healing rolls are usually dice rolls, with the type of dice noted in the spell or effect that provides healing. Some healing rolls will have a static number component, which is added or subtracted from the dice result. Most healing impacts your Grit, but not your hit points, although some hit point healing exists.

Damage and Healing Rolls are gone over in more detail in Chapter 08 - Combat.

<GameplayCard
  color="green"
  title="Damage Roll Example"
>

You are a level 1 fighter, you have a Brawn score of 16, which gives you a +1 to damage rolls with most weapons, and a Combat Bonus of +2. Your Arming Sword deals 1d6 damage. When you successfully deal damage, you roll 1d6 and add 3. This means that when you deal damage, you deal between 4 to 9 damage.

</GameplayCard>

<GameplayCard
  color="green"
  title="Healing Roll Example"
>

You are a level 1 cleric. You cast the Cure Light Wounds spell, which restores 2d6 Grit. This means that by casting this spell, you will allow your target to recover 2-12 grit.
</GameplayCard>

### Saving Throws
Saving Throws are made when your character is attempting to avoid dangerous effects. There are 5 categories of saving throws (Doom, Device, Binding, Breath, and Spell), each an abstract version of what it's trying to do. Saving Throws use your character classes Saving Throw values.

When making a saving throw, you roll a d20, and compare it to your Saving Throw value. If it is greater than or equal to your Saving Throw value, you succeed, and negate (or reduce) the effects that you attempted to save against. It is rare, but your Attributes can impact your saving throws. 

When you gain a bonus to a saving throw (such as a +2 bonus), you add this bonus to the dice roll you make to determine if you make the save or not, likewise, a penalty is subtracted from the dice.

Saving Throws are gone over in more detail in Chapter 08 - Combat.

<GameplayCard
  color="green"
  title="Saving Throw Example"
>
You are a level 1 fighter, and your Save vs Spell is a 15. A magic-user attempts to cast a Charm spell on you, and you are entitled to a Save vs Spell to avoid the effects. You roll a d20, if the result is 15 or higher you avoid the effects of the spell. If you roll a 14 or lower, you become charmed per the spell description.
</GameplayCard>

### Attribute Test
Attribute Tests are used when your character attempts tasks that rely on natural ability. Your attributes represent your raw capability, while your background, ancestry, or class may grant additional training.

To make an Attribute Test, the GM will tell you which Attribute to use. Roll a d20. You succeed if you roll **equal to or lower than your modified Attribute Score**.

Attribute Tests are gone over in more detail in Chapter 07 - Adventuring.

#### Eligibility
Most attribute tests are open to anyone, but some require specific training or background to attempt at all. When an attribute test calls for specific training, your character has one of three levels of Eligibility.

Eligibility is separate from your Skill Bonus. Being Eligible for a test doesn't guarantee your Skill Bonus applies to it, and being only Partially Eligible doesn't prevent your Skill Bonus from applying if you have other training that's actually relevant.

**Fully Eligible**: You may attempt the test normally.

**Partially Eligible**: You may attempt the test, but you treat your Attribute as if it were half its normal value, after modifiers.

**Ineligible**: You cannot attempt the test.

To determine a character's Eligibility, the GM considers their ancestry, background, class, and anything notable they've accomplished during their adventures, and asks how the character would know how to do the task:

- **Fully Eligible** if the character did this regularly, as part of their work or training.
- **Partially Eligible** if the character was around it, saw it done often, or did something closely related.
- **Ineligible** if nothing in the character's life would have taught them this.

Backgrounds describe what a character did, rather than listing every task they can attempt. When in doubt, talk it through. A good reason from the player is worth more than a list.

<GameplayCard
  color="green"
  title="Eligibility Example"
>
The party finds an unfamiliar herb, and the GM calls for a Logic test to identify it.

- The fighter, once an herbalist, is Fully Eligible and applies their Skill Bonus — herbalism is exactly what the test calls for.
- The druid, once an innkeeper, is also Fully Eligible and applies their Skill Bonus — a druid's training gives them a natural affinity for identifying plants. Their time as an innkeeper has nothing to do with it.
- The cleric, once a shoemaker, is Fully Eligible but does not apply their Skill Bonus — shoemaking taught them nothing about herbs, but their clerical training covered basic medicine.
- The thief, once a hunter, is Partially Eligible and does not apply their Skill Bonus — hunters pick up some incidental plant knowledge, but not enough to identify this one confidently.
- The magic-user, once an alchemist's apprentice, is Partially Eligible and applies their Skill Bonus — the apprenticeship covered processed herbs used in potions, but they left before learning to identify herbs in their raw, natural state.
- Another thief, once an innkeeper, is Ineligible — the exact same background as the druid, but with no class training to draw on, it grants nothing here.

Later, the party needs to follow a band of raiders through a forest.

- The barbarian is Fully Eligible — reading trails in the wild is how they survived.
- The fighter, once a hunter, is Fully Eligible — this is exactly what they did before they took up the sword.
- The paladin is Partially Eligible — a knight learns to scout and ride down an enemy, but not to read a trail through brush.
- The cleric, once a shoemaker, is Ineligible — nothing in temple life or the cobbler's shop taught them this.

At a lord's feast, the party must greet a visiting baron without giving offense.

- The paladin is Fully Eligible — noble courtesy is part of a knight's training.
- The cleric is Partially Eligible — temples deal with nobles often enough to learn the basics.
- The fighter and the barbarian are Ineligible — neither their classes nor their backgrounds ever brought them near a lord's table.

</GameplayCard>


#### Modifiers
The GM may apply modifiers to an Attribute Test. All modifiers adjust your Attribute Score for the duration of the test.

A **+X modifier** increases your Attribute Score by X.

A **-X modifier** decreases your Attribute Score by X.

For example, a character with a Precision of 10 who receives a +2 modifier treats their Precision as 12 for that test.

#### Skill Bonus
If your ancestry, background, or class grants training relevant to the task, you may add your **Skill Bonus** to the Attribute Score for the test. Some tasks require such training; characters without it automatically fail regardless of their roll. Skill Bonuses are applied as a Modifier.

<GameplayCard
  color="green"
  title="Attribute Test Example"
>
  You are a 4th-level Thief with a Precision of 13. You attempt to pick a difficult lock. The GM applies a -2 modifier to the test. Because you are a Thief, you also add your +1 Skill Bonus.

Your modified Attribute Score is 12 (13 - 2 + 1), thus you must roll a 12 or lower on a d20 to succeed.
</GameplayCard>

#### Attribute Scores at and Above 20
A d20 can never roll higher than 20, so an Attribute Score of 20 or more would never fail on its own. To keep a chance of failure, a natural 20 is resolved with a second roll.

When your modified Attribute Score is 20 or higher and you roll a natural 20, roll the d20 again. If that second roll is equal to or lower than your Attribute Score minus 19, you succeed after all. Otherwise, the test fails.

Brannoc is a 7th-level Barbarian with a Heartiness of 19, trying to hold his breath through a flooded passage. The GM calls for a Heartiness test, and rules that a barbarian's training in surviving the wilds applies. His +2 Skill Bonus raises his modified Heartiness to 21.

Brannoc rolls a natural 20. Because his score is 21, he rolls the d20 again and needs a 2 or lower (21 - 19). He rolls a 1 and struggles through, surfacing just in time. Had he rolled a 3 or higher, the test would have failed.
</GameplayCard>

#### Attribute Drain

Some undead, poisons, diseases, and curses **drain** your Attributes, lowering the scores themselves. Unlike a penalty to a test, drain changes everything that depends on the drained score until it recovers, such as the damage bonus from Brawn or the Hit Points from Heartiness. Drain never changes your maximum Grit.

* **Drain adds up.** Each time you are drained, your score drops further.
* **Heartiness drain** lowers your maximum Hit Points to match your new Heartiness, and your current Hit Points drop by the same amount. If this reduces your Hit Points to 0, you die.
* **Drained to 0.** If a physical Attribute (Brawn, Heartiness, Agility, or Precision) other than Heartiness reaches 0, you are Paralyzed until it recovers. If a mental Attribute (Logic, Reason, Savvy, or Presence) reaches 0, you are Unconscious until it recovers. If your Heartiness reaches 0, you die.
* **Recovery.** Drain heals slowly with rest. See *Recovering from Attribute Drain* in [Rest and Recovery](/playersguide/07-adventuring/03-rest-and-recovery/#recovering-from-attribute-drain).

### Opposed Attribute Tests
Opposed Attribute Tests are used when two or more creatures directly contest one another. Wrestling for control of a weapon, forcing a door closed against someone pushing from the other side, racing toward the same object, and attempting to deceive an attentive observer can all call for Opposed Attribute Tests.

Each participant makes an Attribute Test using the Attribute selected by the GM. The participants do not necessarily need to test the same Attribute: one creature might use Brawn to hold a door closed while another uses Agility to slip through before it shuts.

Resolve an Opposed Attribute Test in the following order:

1. A participant who succeeds on their Attribute Test defeats a participant who fails.
2. If multiple participants succeed, the participant with the highest successful roll wins.
3. If every participant fails, the existing situation remains unchanged.
4. If the highest successful rolls tie, the existing situation remains unchanged.

A roll that exceeds the tested Attribute is a failure and cannot defeat a successful roll, regardless of how high its result is. Among successful rolls, a higher result represents the stronger performance because it is closer to the limit of what that creature could accomplish.

When the existing situation remaining unchanged would favor one participant, that participant is sometimes called the defender. For example, the current holder keeps an object after a tied attempt to wrest it away, and a creature remains standing after a tied attempt to knock it down.

<GameplayCard
  color="green"
  title="Opposed Attribute Test Example"
>
A Magic-User with 6 Brawn attempts to wrest an object from a warrior with 18 Brawn. Both characters make a Brawn Test.

If the Magic-User rolls an 8, their test fails. If the warrior rolls a 13, the warrior succeeds and retains the object.

If the Magic-User instead rolls a 5, both characters succeed. The warrior's 13 is the higher successful roll, so the warrior still retains the object. The Magic-User could win by rolling a 5 while the warrior rolled a successful 4 or failed with a 19, but the warrior's greater Brawn gives them a much larger range of strong successful results.
</GameplayCard>

### X-in-Y Check
X-inY checks are similar to attribute tests, but is often made when the outcomes aren't based on aptitude, but rather random chance, luck, or other outside forces. Occasionally, your attributes may grant you a specific x-in-y, or alter certain x-in-y chances too.

When you make an x-in-y check, you will roll the dice indicated as Y, and if the result of the dice is X or lower, you succeed. Some attributes or features allow you to alter some X-in_Y checks, but otherwise X-in-Y checks are rarely modified.

<GameplayCard
  color="green"
  title="X-in-Y Check Example"
>
Your party is foraging for food. The GM has determined that it is a 1-in-6 chance to find food. Someone rolls a d6, and if they get a 1, they find food. 

The party also includes a Ranger, who has a feature that allows them to forage for food separately from the rest of the party, and increases their chance to find food by +1-in-Y. Since the party has a 1-in-6 chance, the Ranger also can roll, and they have a 2-in-6 chance to find food (a roll of 1 or 2 on a d6). 
</GameplayCard>

## Proficiency
When dealing with certain rules of the game, the word Proficiency is used a lot. Proficiency means a few things, depending on the context, but the easy answer is "if you are proficient with something, you can use it effectively, and if you are not proficient, you cannot". For example, you must be proficient with armor that you wish to wear, otherwise the penalties outweigh the benefits.

From a game play standpoint, your character will become proficient with certain weapons and armor, based on their class and choices.

### Weapon Proficiency Level
Weapons, and only weapons, have a scaling proficiency level. Rather than just becoming proficient with a weapon, you have varying degrees of proficiency, sometimes called a “Proficiency Level”. See Chapter 4 for additional details on Weapon Proficiency. Outside of becoming Familiar, your Proficiency level offers no direct numeric advantage: Instead, as your proficiency level with weapon groups rise, you gain additional ways to utilize weapons in that group. These additional ways of utilizing weapons can alter how you fight, and lead to ways to attack that lead to increased damage dice.

Proficiency levels in order from worst to best are: Non-Proficient, Familiar, Proficient, Skilled, Expert, Master, Grand Master. Cross-Trained is not a proficiency rank; it represents limited experience carried over from a related weapon group. Proficiency with weapons, armors and shields are gone over in more detail in Chapter 02 - Character Creation, but improving your proficiency level with weapons are gone over in more detail in Chapter 11 - Advancement.

## Static Bonus Types (d20 Rolls)
Some effects grant typed modifiers. The five modifier types are **Skill**, **Magic**, **Situation**, **Ancestry**, and **Condition**. Condition modifiers come from conditions (see [Afflictions and Conditions](/playersguide/07-adventuring/05-afflictions-and-conditions/)).

If multiple modifiers of the same type apply to a roll or Attribute Test, only the highest bonus of that type and the greatest penalty of that type apply. Modifiers of different types stack normally.

A few rare effects grant a modifier with no type, written simply as a bonus or penalty (for example, "a +2 bonus"). Untyped modifiers stack with everything, including other untyped modifiers.

Core statistics are not modifiers. Your Attribute-based bonuses (such as the damage bonus from your Brawn), Combat Bonus, and Saving Throw values are part of your character, and these stacking rules do not apply to them.

Some modifiers are applied directly to a dice roll (such as Attack Rolls and Saving Throws), while others modify the target number of an Attribute Test. In all cases, modifiers follow the same stacking rules above.

### Adding Dice
Occasionally, you will be instructed to add dice to a roll, for example “add an extra dice of damage” or “add an extra dice of healing”, or “add two extra dice of damage”. When you come across this, you roll the added number of dice, in addition to the dice you would have otherwise rolled. For example, if you deal 2d8 points of healing, and a feature allows you to add an extra dice of healing, you roll 3d8.

There should be no occasion where you would have multiple different dice, but if there is, add it to largest listed dice, unless another dice type is more logical.

## Keywords
Keywords are used to define who and what you are. They describe your fundamental nature, ancestry, training, and the experiences that make your character unique.

Spells, magical items, and features often use keywords to determine whether you are a valid target, whether you can utilize them, or whether they are available to you as a character option.

Although many keywords consist of a single word, some may contain multiple words and are always treated as a single keyword, for example "Knight of the Black Lion" is a single keyword.

Keywords are divided into five categories: **Essence, Form, Species, Archetype,** and **Story**. There are light rules that govern each category, but during play your keywords can largely be treated as one list.

### Essence Keywords
<p style="margin-top: 0;"><small><i>Your Nature</i></small></p>

Every creature has exactly one **Essence**, which describes what sustains it and how supernatural forces treat it. Essence is not morality or behavior; it only describes what a creature is. A creature's Essence changes only when the thing keeping it alive changes, such as a mortal rising as a vampire.

Each Essence belongs to one of three **families**. Many spells affect an entire family rather than naming each Essence. When a rule names a family, it applies to every Essence in that family.

<div class="green-table">

| **Essence** | **Family** | **Description** | **Examples** |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| **Aeon** | Living | Legends given flesh, older and greater than mortal life | Dragons, phoenixes, ancient guardians |
| **Animus** | Living | Ordinary living creatures that are born, age, and die | Humans, dwarves, wolves, owlbears |
| **Cosmic** | Living | Alien beings from beyond the world's natural order | Things from beyond the stars |
| **Fey** | Living | Beings bound by custom, place, or oath | Elves, hearth-spirits, the hidden folk |
| **Construct** | Wrought | Beings made by craft or magic rather than born | Golems, animated statues |
| **Elemental** | Wrought | Beings whose bodies are a living force or substance | Living flame, walking stone |
| **Necros** | Severed | Beings sustained by death or undeath | Ghosts, vampires, the walking dead |
| **Umbra** | Severed | Beings born of corruption and malice | Demons, devils, possessing spirits |

</div>

Game Masters can find the full details of each Essence in the [Folio of Fiends](/foliooffiends/01-essences/).

### Form Keywords
<p style="margin-top: 0;"><small><i>Your Body</i></small></p>

Form keywords describe the shape of a creature's body. Your ancestry determines your Form, and every ancestry in this book is **Humanoid**.

Other Forms include **Beast, Monstrosity, Plant, Amorphous, Swarm, Incorporeal,** and **Object**. You will mostly meet these on the creatures you face, and in the spells and features that target them.

### Species Keywords
<p style="margin-top: 0;"><small><i>Your Heritage</i></small></p>

Species keywords identify what kind of creature something is. Your ancestry determines which Species keywords you possess. If your heritage spans more than one people, you possess the Species keywords of each; a half-orc is both **Human** and **Orc**.

Creatures you encounter often have more than one Species keyword, such as a broad group and their own kind. A horse is both **Equine** and **Horse**, so a spell that affects **Equine** creatures affects it, as does a feature that names **Horse**.

<GameplayCard
  color="green"
  title="Keywords Example"
>

You are a dwarf fighter. Your keywords are *Animus, Humanoid, Dwarf, Soldier*. A spell that targets **Humanoid** creatures can target you, and so can one that targets the **Animus** Essence or the **Living** family. A feature that only affects **Elf** creatures does not.

</GameplayCard>

### Archetype Keywords
<p style="margin-top: 0;"><small><i>Your Training</i></small></p>

Archetype keywords describe what you have trained to do. Your class grants them.

There are four Archetype keywords, and each is shared by one or more classes:

<div class="green-table">

| **Archetype Keyword** | **Description** |
|:--|:--|
| **Adept** | Trained in skill, cunning, and specialized knowledge |
| **Arcanist** | Studied in the magical arts |
| **Priest** | Serves a divine or natural power |
| **Soldier** | Trained for battle |

</div>

If you are a multiclass character, you possess the Archetype keywords of each of your classes.

### Story Keywords
<p style="margin-top: 0;"><small><i>Your Journey</i></small></p>

Story keywords describe what has happened to you. Unlike other keywords, you usually gain them during play. They can come from curses, oaths, supernatural transformations, joining an order, class features, and occasionally the Game Master. Some classes grant Story keywords as soon as you take them; a paladin gains **Paladin** and **Hallowed**, and a druid gains **Druid**.

A Story keyword lasts as long as the story behind it does. A character whose curse is lifted loses the **Cursed** keyword, and a knight who leaves their order loses that order's keyword.

For example, you might gain **Cursed** after being afflicted by an ancient malediction, or **Knights of the Black Lion** when you join that order. Campaign-specific keywords are usually Story keywords as well, and may grant unique benefits or opportunities during play.

#### Hallowed and Defiled

**Hallowed** and **Defiled** are Story keywords that describe a supernatural condition impressed upon a creature, object, or place.

A creature with the **Hallowed** keyword has been consecrated, transformed, or personally invested with sacred power. Sacred power does not merely pass through such a creature; it has become part of the creature's supernatural nature.

Religious devotion alone does not grant the Hallowed keyword. Clerics, Theurges, and other priests may serve divine powers, perform sacred rites, and cast clerical spells without themselves being Hallowed. They wield or channel sacred power, but are not necessarily changed by it.

Paladins possess the **Hallowed** keyword because they have been personally invested with power. A paladin is not merely a servant or practitioner of the faith, but a living vessel of its authority. Other creatures may gain the **Hallowed** keyword through consecration, divine intervention, transformation, or similar supernatural events.

A creature with the **Defiled** keyword has likewise been personally marked, corrupted, desecrated, or transformed by profane power. Ordinary cruelty, wickedness, or opposition to a religion is not enough to make a creature Defiled.

A creature of any Essence may be **Hallowed** or **Defiled**. Just as an **Animus** Paladin is **Hallowed**, an **Aeon** dragon may be **Defiled**, and a **Necros** ancestral guardian may possess neither keyword. Although many **Umbra** and **Necros** creatures are Defiled, their Essence does not automatically make them so.

Unless a rule states otherwise, a creature may possess neither keyword, but cannot possess both simultaneously. **Hallowed** and **Defiled** describe what supernatural power has done to a creature, not the choices it has made. A wicked tyrant may be neither, and an innocent victim of a dark rite may be **Defiled**.

### Creature Interactions
Some spells and features interact differently with creatures possessing specific keywords. When this is the case, the spell or feature will include a **Creature Interactions** section describing those differences.

If a spell or feature does not contain a **Creature Interactions** section, it affects all creatures normally.

Some spells and features use the terms **Valid Creature** or **Eligible Creature**. These terms are defined by the feature's **Creature Interactions** section. Unless otherwise noted, only creatures listed as Valid or Eligible Creatures are affected by that feature or spell.

Creature Interactions may grant additional effects, modify existing effects, or define exceptions based on a creature's keywords. Creatures not listed in a feature's Creature Interactions section are unaffected unless otherwise noted.

## Other Rules
There are many other rules in ITD&TD, each mentioned in their own section, however, there are some additional rules that should be gone over here, to help understand all other rules.

### Round Down
Sometimes, you will need to do some division. If you do so, and end up with a fraction, you round down, even if the result is one-half or greater.

### Specific Beats General
Lots of rules are presented in this book. However, some rules break generalized rules in some way. If a specific rule contradicts a general rule, the specific rule wins.

### Time and Play Records
Although you may only play with 4-6 people at a time, the game is meant for large groups to all be playing in the same world, though not necessarily at the same time. The passage of time should be tracked. Characters should age, and years should go by during campaigns.

A good rule of thumb is that for every 1 day in real life that you don't play, 1 day in game-time happens. Characters should be investing in Downtime Events for these periods of no adventuring. GMs and players should work out what happens during these times before or after a session, or even between sessions. These should be well recorded for other players to get a sense of what's going on (unless it should be a secret!).

Sometimes, you may need time to speed up or slow down. For example, adventuring in the winter may simply be too dangerous, and all players agree that they would rather wait for late winter or early spring to start up again. Go ahead and simply roll the calendar forward several months. If players had particular goals during this time, you could always handle them on the side.

Alternatively, you may slow the campaign, or halt "in-game" time completely for some outside situations.

Regardless, it should be well communicated with all players.

### Digital Age
It has never been easier to keep in contact with groups of people. I recommend a Discord Server made specifically for tracking your game, although email groups, Facebook groups, google groups, slack, and a vast amount of other products can help communicate with each other.

However, a GM may ask you not to use devices at the table, unless it is clear that you are using it to play the game. This should be figured out in Session 0, but some GMs prefer paper character sheets with physical dice, and others are okay with digital character sheets.

### Playing with Others
This is a cooperative game! As such, it's important to keep your fellow players in mind as you play!

#### Character Maiming and Death
Playing ITD&TD means playing with rule sets that do not set your characters up as super heroes, but rather mundane creatures who are only slightly better than those around them. They are still prone to getting injured or killed. Many players get attached to characters, and wish to not see them hurt or killed, especially in long campaigns. Steel yourself, for playing this game likely means you will need to deal with some of your characters getting seriously maimed or killed.

Try to keep an open mind! This can be very good for the story you and your friends are trying to tell. It can be hard to lose a character you've grown fond of, but this can be the nature of the game. 

#### Respect
Please be respectful of each other as you play. Some topics which may be okay for you may not be for others. Hash out what is and isn't acceptable before you play (Session 0 is the best place!) and remember that there is no wrong way to play, but there are vast enough differences where people may simply not agree to play well together.

#### Player vs Player
Make sure to discuss how to handle player-vs-player at your table before play starts. Many tables have different ways of handling it. Some allow it, others don’t. Some have a hybrid system, and some only allow it when it makes sense. It is recommended that player-vs-player content simply not be allowed at your table.

But if you feel that player-vs-player content should be allowed, try using the following rules: If player-vs-player comes up, the “target” of the action simply gets to chose the outcome. If a player thief is attempting to steal from them, the victim gets to decide if the attempt was successful, if it failed, or leave it to the dice if they wish. This allows players not interested in player-vs-player to have a kind of “opt-out” function, and allows players who want to engage the ability to “opt-in”.

Regardless, player-vs-player actions should be handled in-session, and with all players knowledge. Engaging in player-vs-player behavior in down-time content should require all players be aware and agree to participate.

Regardless of if you use the recommended method, or another method, make sure all players are aware how your table will handle player vs player outcomes.

#### Good Faith
This game works best when playing with others that have a good faith interpretation of the rules, and all players at the table should indulge in such. Players attempting to "game" the system for advantage, GMs who abuse rules for "revenge", and power gamers who take a rule so literal to gain an advantage that it ruins other players fun are all acting in bad faith. There are many TTRPG horror stories out there; remember that this is a cooperative game.
