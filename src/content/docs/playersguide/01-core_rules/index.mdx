## How do you play this game?

The basic rules of play are simple. One Player takes the roll of the Game Master ("GM"), who acts as part referee, part storyteller. All other players, usually between 3-5 people, take the roll of Player Characters ("PC" or "Character"). The GM will describe an environment your character is in, adversaries they are up against, or situations your character finds themselves in, and you tell the GM what your character wishes to do. The GM will then describe what happens or may leave the outcome to a dice roll.

The worlds that ITD&TD present should be very dangerous, and it is highly recommended that when you create your Character, you create a character who is willing to adventure, and willing to be part of a group of allies, often called a "Party". Characters thinking of themselves as "lone wolves" or "not needing help" don't last long in this dangerous world.

>### An Example of Play
>
> **Richard (GM)**: Rain drums steadily on the tavern roof as you all sit around a rough wooden table in the Flickering Flagon. The innkeep has just told you about the missing miller’s daughter and the strange lights in the barrow hills. What do you do?
>
> **Sarah (Astril)**: Astril folds her arms and asks the innkeep, “When did the lights first appear?”
>
> **Richard (GM)**: He says it started two nights ago—just before the girl vanished, but you notice that he looks nervous, like he regrets saying anything at all.
>
> **Maria (Brynjolf)**: Brynjolf stands up and says “Then we don’t wait. Storm or not, we head to these hills.”
>
> **James (Cerrig)**: Cerrig doesn’t move yet. I want to watch the innkeep—see if he’s hiding something.
>
> **Richard (GM)**: That sounds good, can you roll a Reason Test? You're a thief, so go ahead and apply your Skill Bonus.
>
> **James (Cerrig)**: *(James rolls a d20, gets a 11. He applies his -1 bonus from his Skill Bonus, bringing to a 10)* "oh, that was close, my score is 11, and I rolled an 11, but with my Skill Bonus, I get a 10 instead, that's a success, right?"
>
> **Richard (GM)**: It is, and you notice his eyes flick briefly to a hooded figure in the corner before he answers any question.
>
> **James (Cerrig)**: Oh, I don’t like that. Cerrig's going to see if he recognizes the hooded figure.
>

> **Ronnie (Derrian)**: "Does Derrian notice Cerrig's glance?"

> **Richard (GM)**: "James, was Cerrig trying to hide it, or was he pretty obvious about his glance?"

## Basic Rules

After describing what you wish your character would do, the GM will narrate the results. Often times, the outcome of this narration may be determined by a dice roll. There are several types of dice rolls that may need to be made in ITD&TD. ***Attack Rolls, Saving Throws, Attribute Test***, and ***x-in-y***.

### Attack Rolls

Attack Rolls are made when you are attempting to strike a creature with a weapon, or an unarmed strike. Attack rolls might also be made as part of wrestling attempts, archery competitions, or even attempts to do tricky combat maneuvers. Attack rolls will use your character classes Combat Bonus.

When making an attack roll, you will roll a d20, add the relevant modifiers, and compare the result to the Armor Class of the target. If your total roll is equal to or higher than the target's Armor Class, you succeed, and can deal damage with your weapon.

>[!example] Attack Roll Example
>
>You are a level 1 fighter, you have a Brawn score of 16, which gives you a +1 to attack rolls with most weapons, and a combat bonus of +2. To make an attack roll, you roll a d20 and add 3. If the target of your attack had an AC of 12, you would need to roll at least a 9 on the D20 (9+3 = 12) to succeed at dealing damage to the target.

>[!level2] Saving Throws

Saving Throws are made when your character is attempting to avoid dangerous effects. There are 5 categories of saving throws (Death\Poison, Rod\Staff\Wand, Petrification\Paralysis, Breath Weapon, and Spell), each an abstract version of what it's trying to do. Saving Throws use your character classes Saving Throw values.

When making a saving throw, you roll a d20, and compare it to your Saving Throw value. If it is greater than or equal to your Saving Throw value, you succeed, and negate (or reduce) the effects that you attempted to save against.

>[!example] Saving Throw Example
>
>You are a level 1 fighter, and your Save vs Spell is a 15. A magic-user attempts to cast a Charm spell on you, and you are entitled to a Save vs Spell to avoid the effects. You roll a d20, if the result is 15 or higher you avoid the effects of the spell. If you roll a 14 or lower, you become charmed per the spell description.

>[!level2] Attribute Test

Attribute Tests are made when your character is attempting to do almost anything in the world. You are simply making a test against your natural abilities, although your background and choice of class can grant you a bonus. Attributes Tests will use your Attributes, but you may be able to apply your class Skill Bonus if your ancestry, background, or class allows it.

When you make an attribute test, you roll a d20, adding any penalties, or subtracting any bonuses the GM may ascribed to the task. Additionally, if your ancestry, background, or class grants you a bonus, you can subtract your class's Skill Bonus from the roll. Compare the end result to the attribute being tested; if it's the same or lower, you succeed.

>[!example] Attribute Test Example
>
>You are a level 4 thief, and your Precision attribute is 13. You are attempting to pick a lock. The GM has decided that this lock is difficult, and ascribes a +2 penalty to the test. However, since you are a thief, you can apply your -1 Skill Bonus to this test as well. You roll a d20, and get a 13. Adding the 2 points from the penalty, your result would have been 15, even your -1 Skill Bonus (14) doesn't quite bring you to a succeed. You would have needed to roll at least a 12 to succeed at this test.

>[!level2] X-in-Y Check

X-inY checks are similar to attribute tests, but is often made when the outcomes aren't based on aptitude, but rather random chance, luck, or other outside forces. Occasionally, your attributes may grant you a specific x-in-y, or alter certain x-in-y chances too.

When you make an x-in-y check, you will roll the dice indicated as Y, and if the result of the dice is X or lower, you succeed.

>[!example] X-in-Y Example
>
>You are foraging for food. The GM has determined that it is a 1-in-6 chance to find food. You roll a d6, and if you get a 1, you find food. The GM has also decided that the ranger can attempt this test separately, but has a 2-in-6 chance of finding good. They also roll a d6, but they succeed on a 1 or a 2.

>[!level2] Proficiency

When dealing with certain rules of the game, the word Proficiency is used a lot. Proficiency means a few things, depending on the context, but the easy answer is "if you are proficient with something, you can use it effectively." For example, you must be proficient with armor that you wish to wear.

From a game play standpoint, your character will become proficient with certain weapons and armor, based on their class and choices.

>[!level3] Weapon Proficiency Level

Weapons, and only weapons, have a scaling proficiency level. Rather than just becoming proficient with a weapon, you have varying degrees of proficiency, sometimes called a “Proficiency Level”. See Chapter 4 for additional details on Weapon Proficiency.

Your Proficiency level offers no direct numeric advantage: Instead, as your proficiency level with weapon groups rise, you gain additional ways to utilize weapons in that group. These additional ways of utilizing weapons can alter how you fight, and lead to increased damage dice.

>[!level2] Static Bonus Types

Bonuses are added to d20 rolls when you make them. And there are a fair number of different types of bonuses. When you have multiple bonus types, they add (or subtract!) together. However, if you have multiple sources of the same bonus type, you simply use the highest result. Thus, if you have a +1 magic bonus and a +2 magic bonus, you apply only the +2. However, if you have a +1 Ancestry bonus and a +2 Magic bonus, you gain a +3 to the roll in question.

>[!level2] Adding Dice

Occasionally, you will be instructed to add dice to a roll, for example “add an extra dice of damage” or “add an extra dice of healing”, or “add two extra dice of damage”. When you come across this, you roll the added number of dice, in addition to the dice you would have otherwise rolled. For example, if you deal 2d8 points of healing, and a feature allows you to add an extra dice of healing, you roll 3d8.

There should be no occasion where you would have multiple different dice, but if there is, add it to largest listed dice, unless another dice type is more logical.
