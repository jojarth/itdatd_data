There are 8 Attributes in ITD&TD: Brawn, Heartiness, Agility, Precision, Logic, Reason, Savvy, and Presence.

Each attribute governs your characters natural abilities in that attribute. Most creatures have attributes between 8 and 12 in each attribute score, with 9 representing the average human person.

### Roll for Attributes
To determine your Attributes roll 4d6, and discard the lowest dice. This leaves you with 3d6 remaining, generating a number between 3 and 18. This is your character's Brawn Score. You then perform this action again, leading to your Heartiness Score. Repeat for Agility, Precision, Logic, Reason, Savvy, and Presence in that order. This is called "4d6 down the line".

Once you've determined your Attributes, note them, along with the various bonuses they provide based on the tables listed in the Attribute.

:::note[I don't assign my attributes?]{icon="information"}
The spirit of this game is to generate a random adventurer, rather than a character ahead of time. This is antithetical to what other TTRPGs do, allowing you to generate a character that is yours.

The way you role play switches around a little. You now have a block of abilities, and you need to come up with a character, rather than having a character concept that you put into play. Although subtile, this leads to a different perception of your character\characters.

ITD&TD is often much more lethal than other TTRPGs, especially at lower levels. Having a random adventurer blunts the blow of losing a character. At the same token, it allows gameplay to be a larger influence on the character's story.
:::
  
## What are the attributes?
Each attribute governs a creature's natural abilities in the ways listed below.

### Brawn
Strength of limb, muscle, and raw might. This attribute governs when feats of strength need to be called upon. High Brawn characters tend to be strong, and thus tend to be more suited to wade into combat, swinging large weapons, breaking down doors, and lifting heavy objects. Low Brawn characters tend to be weak, struggling with heavy loads, unable to effectively use heavier equipment, and easily pushed aside in combat.

### Heartiness
Stamina, endurance, and resistance to effects against the body. This attribute governs when your toughness is called upon. High Heartiness characters tend to be resilient, and thus have a higher capacity to receive damage, shrug off illness, and are not easily fatigued. Low Heartiness characters tend to be frail, sickly, or delicate. They often succumb to illness, and are quicker to tire.

### Agility
Quickness of step, physical reflexes, and swiftness in battle. This attribute governs your ability to move your body quickly. High Agility characters tend to be quick and nimble, able to perform feats of acrobatics, avoidance, and other forms of body control. Low Agility characters tend to be slow and lumbering, stumbling when trying to move quickly, dropping things, and unable to keep their balance.

### Precision
A steady hand, keen eye, and fine control of movement. This attribute governs your ability to perform work that requires exactitude. High Precision characters tend to be good with their hands, good marksmen, precise with a light blade, and are able to tinker with their hands. Low Precision characters tend to be unsteady and careless, unable to aim properly, unsuited to detailed work, and producing poor craftsmanship.

### Logic
Lore, memory, and ordered thought. This attribute governs your ability to learn and retain knowledge, your memory, and unraveling puzzles. High Logic characters tend to be able to recall and process information, solve puzzles, and master complex magical spells. Low Logic characters tend to be dull and forgetful, confusing instructions, misremembering details, and unable to recall information accurately.

Many functions of Logic can be improved upon via downtime events. For example, you may start as a character who cannot read and write, and only speaks one language. However, if you are willing to put in the downtime and coin, you can end up with a character who can speak well beyond what is offered in the tables below, as well as reading and writitng more than just your native language. More information on languages and literacy can be found in chapter 7.

:::tip[Literacy in ITD&TD]{icon="information"}
  Literacy is low in ITD&TD, most characters cannot read or write. This is by design, and being able to read and write is considered a high-value skill.

  There are some ancestries and classes that can interact with your Logic to determine your known languages; for example if your Logic is 8 or higher, and you are a Cleric, you become literate in one of your native languages; and if your Logic is 16 or higher, you become literate in all native languages you speak.
:::

### Reason
Discernment, judgment, and clarity of mind. This attribute governs your ability to utilize your intuition and senses to process information. High Reason characters tend to be wise and discerning, seeing through lies, trusting their “gut”, and being highly perceptive. Low Reason characters tend to be foolish and rash, easily swayed by conspiracy, leaping to incorrect conclusions, and missing details.

## Unplayable Characters
Generating a character should be generating an adventurer. With 4d6 drop the lowest, it should be rare that a character is unplayable. If you rolled any of the following, your character is considered unplayable, and you have the option of scraping the character and recreating. You reroll all attributes from the top.

* If you rolled any Attribute of a 3.
* If you have 2 or more Attributes that are 6 or lower.
* If you have no Attributes greater than 11.

A GM may have additional provisions, restrictions, or ideas on how to fix it.

## Altering Ability Scores
After selecting your ancestry in Step 2, you will roll for your starting age. This is quite an important step, as your starting age can have an impact on your attributes. Most characters will start play as young, or prime characters, but occasionally you might see a mature character at the start of play.

After rolling your starting age, your age will present how you adjust your attributes. Note that some of these adjustments are optional, where others are not. For example, a young elf is allowed the option to increase their Agility, Precision, or Logic by 1, at the cost of reducing their Brawn, Heartiness, or Reason by 1 too. This is a choice, they can elect not to take these bonuses and penalties. However, an elf in their Prime is required to select two attributes from Agility, Precision, Logic, and Presence, and increase the selected attributes by 1 point each.

After rolling your age, you apply all age ability score modifiers in order from young (the order is young > prime > mature > aging > elderly). Starting age adjustments will not allow attributes to go above 19 or below 6; however if you reach said age naturally while adventuring you can make said adjustments (you must have adventured for at least 1 year "in game" before being allowed to make such an adjustment).

If an adjustment would take an attribute above 19 or below a 6, and it's an optional adjustment, you must make a different choice. If it is not an optional adjustment, simply ignore it. In our elf example above, if they start play with a 6 heartiness and 17 logic, they can increase their logic to 18, but cannot select heartiness as the attribute to reduce to make this adjustment.

:::note[Rolling for Age?]{icon="information"}

The default rules of the game is for your characters attributes, age, height, and weight to be randomly rolled. However, many GMs find that players might wish to simply determine these factors, rather than roll them randomly.

It is highly recommended that age is rolled, as ancestrial attribute adjustments are tied to age. Height and weight can be randomly rolled, or simply selected by the player of the character.

If you wish to allow characters to select their starting age you can! But be prepared for players to select the age which would grant them the most benefit to their character with the least harmful penalties. This is one such instance where "min-maxing" a character could be applied.
:::
