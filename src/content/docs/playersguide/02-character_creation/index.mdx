title: Character Creation
---
The first thing you need to do before adventuring into the dangerous worlds of ITD&TD is create an adventurer or two of your own, often called a Character. Sometimes we refer to player created characters as simply "Player Characters" or "PCs".

A character is a combination of in game statistics and calculations, and your imagination. You might be a stalwart hero, ready to slay foes with a sword, or an aged hero who still has some tricks to learn or some fight left in him. Although you must abide by the game rules, your imagination has no limits.

  :::tip[Attributes and Characters]{icon="information"}
The difference in Attribute Bonuses are small, especially in combat. Thus, you do not need a perfect set of attributes to create a character.

If you follow along the example characters, you will notice that Maria's character Brynjolf would likely make a better Cleric than a Fighter, but Maria didn't want to play a Cleric. In fact, by attributes alone, Brynjolf would have made a better Cleric than Sarah's character, Astril, who is a Cleric because that's what Sarah wanted to play. Neither of these characters are inefficient at their classes, however.

While creatures with higher attributes are certainly at an advantage, it's not so great that playing an "unoptimized" character will impact your enjoyment too much.
  :::

### Character Sheets

While going through this document, it is assumed you use the official character sheet. However, a character sheet can be anything. People will make and share their own; people may make digital tools for tracking; and other people still will just use scrap paper.

Regardless of how you track your character, there is a lot to track, and some organization to your way of doing so is recommended.

### Keywords

Keywords are simply used for identifying how certain affects will effect a creature. You will gain 2 types of keywords during character creation: ancestral keywords and class keywords. Backgrounds do not offer keywords.

  :::tip[Why Keywords?]{icon="information"}

Keywords are a quick and easy way to identify certain aspects of a creature to determine how magic affects them. Instead of ambiguous "is this creature a humanoid or a fey?" type questions, keywords directly map what a creature is.

Keywords also interact with magical items, certain magical items might only be usable by creatures with certain keywords.

Keywords also exist for future projects within the realm of ITD&TD, which is why you might find some odd choices for the wording of certain keywords. This is to future-proof this system.

  :::

### Number of Characters

As the campaign is meant to simulate the lives of an adventurer, most players will play more than one character across a campaign. You will likely not play more than one at a time, but you will likely have a pool of characters, deciding which one will go along for the adventure. It could be based on other characters coming on that adventure, it could be based on needing an expert trap finder, and you happen to have one, you could just feel like playing a different character, or your other character could be recovering from injury.

Some players will like creating their characters ahead of time, others will wait until specific times to create additional characters, there is no wrong answer. However, your GM may have rules at the table for multiple characters, and as such, they should be hashed out ahead of time, preferably during a Session 0.

####  Character Death

The life of an adventurer is extremely dangerous, and even the most seasoned ones may finally meet their match. Be prepared for characters to die. Although with good choices, good planning, and some luck, your character can make it to higher levels.

There are smart decisions to be made all over the place that will help even the odds. Careful adventures live longer. Characters that work well with others do too.

#### More than one at a time

Occasionally, either due to player count, or other outside factors, you may end up playing more than one of your characters at a time. A GM must approve this, but if you find yourself in this situation, try not to overshadow other players at your table. For example, if there were 3 players, and you are 2 characters to round out the party to 4, you should still only have about 1/3 of the play time dedicated to you, despite playing half the party.

It's about everyone having fun at the table.

#### Hirelings and Henchmen

As you progress, you may find your character needing extra muscle in the dungeon. Loot is heavy, bearing a torch becomes cumbersome, and the more minds trying to discern how a trap works the better. Characters will find themselves likely engaged with Hirelings and Henchmen.

Hirelings are non-adventuring types who are seeking to make a little extra pocket money. They are likely paid a flat rate, and perform simple tasks: carry treasure, cook for the camp, carry a torch etc... Hirelings are controlled by the GM exclusively. Hirelings do not have adventuring class levels (or have low-levels in such a class), and will not likely participate in combat.

Henchmen are different. They can be hired, or found. Henchmen have adventuring class levels. Henchmen also differ from hirelings in many other ways, it is likely that once you obtain one, they are around for the long haul. A player will be allowed to control the henchmen in combat (and limitedly out of combat), but the GM will have tools that may allow them to override player choice in the morale and loyalty scores. Henchmen have a chance to become a player character; either temporarily or permanently.

More about hirelings and henchmen can be found in Chapter 6.

## Creation Steps

If you're already familiar with creating a character, here are the quick steps in the order we recommend creating a character in:

1. Determine attributes
2. Determine your ABCs (Ancestry, Background, Class)
3. Select starting equipment
4. Select spells
5. Finalize gameplay math
6. Finalize roleplay

## Step 1. Determine Attributes

There are 8 Attributes in ITD&TD: Brawn, Heartiness, Agility, Precision, Logic, Reason, Savvy, and Presence.

Each attribute governs your characters natural abilities in that attribute. Most creatures have attributes between 8 and 12 in each attribute score, with 9 representing the average human person.

### Roll for Attributes

To determine your Attributes roll 4d6, and discard the lowest dice. This leaves you with 3d6 remaining, generating a number between 3 and 18. This is your character's Brawn Score. You then perform this action again, leading to your Heartiness Score. Repeat for Agility, Precision, Logic, Reason, Savvy, and Presence in that order. This is called "4d6 down the line".

Once you've determined your Attributes, note them, along with the various bonuses they provide based on the tables listed in the Attribute.

  :::note[I don't assign my attributes?]{icon="information"}

The spirit of this game is to generate a random adventurer, rather than a character ahead of time. This is antithetical to what other TTRPGs do, allowing you to generate a character that is yours.

The way you role play switches around a little. You now have a block of abilities, and you need to come up with a character, rather than having a character concept that you put into play. Although subtile, this leads to a different perception of your character\characters.

ITD&TD is often much more lethal than other TTRPGs, especially at lower levels. Having a random adventurer blunts the blow of losing a character. At the same token, it allows gameplay to be a larger influence on the character's story.
  :::

### Unplayable Characters

Generating a character should be generating an adventurer. With 4d6 drop the lowest, it should be rare that a character is unplayable. If you rolled any of the following, your character is considered unplayable, and you have the option of scraping the character and recreating. You reroll all abilities from the top.

* If you rolled any Attribute of a 3.
* If you have 2 or more Attributes that are 6 or lower.
* If you have no Attributes greater than 11.

A GM may have additional provisions, restrictions, or ideas on how to fix it.

### Altering Ability Scores

While creating a character, you have a few options for adjusting attribute scores before play.

#### Starting Age

A character's starting age can also impact your attributes. During Step 2. Determining your ABCs, you will select an ancestry. Each ancestry has a table that shows you how to roll for your starting age, and what attribute adjustments you make for each age category. You apply all adjustments up to the age you are.

Starting age adjustments will not allow attributes to go above 19 or below 6; however if you reach said age naturally while adventuring you can make said adjustments (you must have adventured for at least 1 year "in game" before being allowed to make such an adjustment).

Your starting age is based on your class keywords.

  :::note[Rolling for Age?]{icon="information"}

The default rules of the game is for your characters attributes, age, height, and weight to be randomly rolled. However, many GMs find that players might wish to simply determine these factors, rather than roll them randomly.

It is highly recommended that age is rolled, due to it's interactions with the game world. Height and weight can be randomly rolled, or simply selected by the player of the character.

If you wish to allow characters to select their starting age you can! But be prepared for players to select the age which would grant them the most benefit to their character with the least harmful penalties. This is one such instance where "min-maxing" a character could be applied.
  :::

## Step 2. Determine your ABCs.
Your character starts play as the sum of their past, made up of their Ancestry, Background, and Class. Ancestry is what determines what species and region a character is from. Background is what determines what you grew up as before becoming an adventure. Class is what defines you as an adventurer.

Your ABCs provide a fundamental starting point for your character, but the true depth of your character shines in gameplay.

### Select your Ancestry

After rolling for Attributes, one of the most impactful decisions you can make (and indeed the next one on our course of creating a character) is your ancestry.

Your ancestry will tell you how your character interacts and thinks about the world. The playable ancestries of ITD&TD are: *Elf, Dwarf, Half-Elf, Halfling, Half-Orc, Human* and *Gnome*. Each ancestry is detailed in Chapter 02, but have the following things you will need to note.

#### Ancestral Keywords

Your ancestry will contain 3-4 ancestral keywords that give you an idea on what kind of creature you are. Keywords are used to determine what can and cannot affect a creature.

  :::tip[Keyword Examples]{icon="information"}

The Hold Person spell only affects creatures who have the Animus and Humanoid keywords. It would function on any creature that has both of these keywords, but not on a creature with the Necros and Humanoid keywords.
  :::
  
#### Ancestry Features

All ancestries will have a few features that they are able to take advantage of: for example, all elves are immune to the paralyzing touch of undead creatures.

Note: not all features are positive, regardless, you must write down and abide by all Ancestry features.

#### Class Keyword Feature

There are 4 class Keywords: *Adept, Arcanist, Priest*, and *Soldier*. Once you select a class, you gain the benefits of the Class Keyword feature based on the class's keyword. Think of your class keyword feature as just an extra ancestry feature you gain, but that is tailored to specific classes.

If you gain more than one class keyword (either the class has multiple class keywords, or you are a multi-class character), you must select only one of the keywords to gain the feature from, and it must be for a keyword that the class gives.

#### Ancestral Information

Very last is a few role-play items for your ancestry. Including normal height\weight ranges, age breakpoints, and methods of generating these items randomly.

### Select a Background

After selecting a race, you should select a background for your character. A background is a profession that your character did, or trained in before becoming an adventurer. When Attribute Tests come up, your background may allow you to apply your Class Skill Bonus in part or whole to the test. Chapter 7 contains additional information on Attribute Tests.

Effectively, your selected background should only influence what kinds of things your character is or was good at.

  :::caution[Background Restrictions]{icon="information"}

Few backgrounds should be tied to your racial selection, although they do exist. For example, elves would ***never*** allow a member of another race to be a tree keeper.

Often, a list of Backgrounds can be found in the campaign guide if you're playing in one. However, you should be able to take any mediaeval type of profession, and call it a background. You should work with your GM in understanding what kinds of backgrounds are allowed.
  :::

  :::danger[Background Power]{icon="information"}

A background should never allow a character to participate in all skill tests. The goal is for characters to all have something to contribute, but never overtake other characters in specialized fields.

For example, if someone attempted to select "Royalty" as a background, they shouldn't immediately gain all the benefits a royal person would indeed actually have. They are an adventurer now, after all. However, they should be allowed to apply their class skill bonus to attribute tests made when things a royal might have learned come into play (diplomacy, history, horseback riding, and courtly manners all come to mind).
  :::
  
### Select a Class

Next, you must make the most important decision of your character's career: your class. A class is a broad definition of abilities and features that define how your character interacts with the world from a game-mechanics standpoint, although it has a large impact on how you role play as well.

Perhaps the most defining feature of your character, your class offers a vast array of benefits. Each class will have a Class Gameplay section, a Class Table and a Class Features section. Classes that gain the ability to cast spells will also have a Class Spell Casting section.

#### Class Gameplay Section

Your class gameplay section contains several sub-sections that will include information such as any requirements that a created character may need to enter the class (attribute or ancestry), specialized rules for playing that class, and your starting weapon familiarities, and armor and shield proficiencies, as well as any other items you need to know about playing a character of this class.

##### Class Keywords

There are 4 class Keywords: *Adept, Arcanist, Priest*, and *Soldier*. These keywords not only tell you a lot about what the class can do, but certain effects in the game only affect creatures with certain keywords. Class Keywords tend to influence what kind of magic can affect a person, almost exclusively with regard to Magical Items.

In addition, your ancestry grants you an extra ancestry feature based on your classes keyword. If your selected class has multiple class keywords, you must only select one ancestry keyword feature, but can select it from any of the ancestry keyword features that your class grants you.

##### Class Requirements

Some classes have certain attribute requirements, or ancestral requirements to play. If none are listed, then anyone can enter that class. If any requirements are listed, you must meet them in order to enter the class.

##### Special Rules

Some classes have specialized rules for playing the class. If such a field is listed, you must abide but the specialized rules listed herein.

##### Starting Weapon Familiarities

Each class starts with a proficiency level of familiar with a small subset of weapons. As part of character creation, you will be granted a number of Weapon Proficiency Points to spend on weapon proficiencies. You can use these Weapon Proficiency Points to upgrade your character's proficiency level with any weapon groups, up to the maximum allowed by their class Offense Bonus.

##### Armor and Shield Proficiency

Each class will tell you what armor you can wear, and shields you can wield. If you wear armor or wield shields that aren't allowed by your class, you may be subject to penalties (see Chapter 5 for more details).

**Note**: Some classes grant you proficiency with armor and shields, but class features cannot function while wearing them.

  :::danger[Armor Proficency Conundrum]{icon="information"}

Magic-Users are a prime example of this conundrum. Magic-Users are proficient with medium armor, as well as buckler and small shields. However, their spells cannot be cast while wearing any armor, or while wielding a shield. Effectively, a magic-user has the option to don his armor when he is out of spells, or if he feels like he's going to enter a dangerous situation and isn't sure he will cast his spells. He can also selectively use a shield, stowing it when he casts a spell at the expense of some actions.
  :::
  
#### Class Table

Your Class table will help tell you how your character interacts with gameplay mechanics of the world. It is a table that contains the various mechanical numbers your character will apply during the game, based on your character level.

Your class table includes the following items on them:

##### Level

Look for your level on this line, and you will find information required to play your character.

##### Experience to Reach Level (XP)

XP is the minimum amount of experience you are required to have to be that level. Once you reach this experience threshold, your character is eligible to get training to become the new level.

##### Combat Proficiency (Combat)

Combat Proficiency is the number you apply to certain situations when in combat. For example, your Combat Proficiency is applied to attack and damage rolls made with weapons you are familiar with, and the damage reduction applied by your Shield block.

##### Skill Bonus (Skill)

A Skill Bonus is a modifier applied when you make Attribute Tests. They have no impact on X-in-Y tests. When you make an attribute test, if you are allowed to apply your Skill Bonus, add your skill bonus to your attribute to determine your target number. 

You do not apply your Skill Bonus to all Attribute Tests, but only tests where your Ancestry, Background, or Class would lend credibility to the test. For example, if you are testing Logic to recall information about a spell being tested, a Magic-User would be allowed to apply their skill bonus, but not a fighter. The GM will interpret when you can and cannot apply your Skill Bonus, but should be open to your input.

##### Saving Throw against Death and Poison (Death)

When you must make a saving throw against effects that might cause instant death, or saving throws against effects that cause poison, you must make this saving throw. To succeed, you must roll higher than the number listed on the saving throw for your level. Some ancestries may alter this saving throw, reducing the target number.

##### Saving Throw against Petrification and Paralysis (PP)

##### Saving Throw against Breath Attacks (Breath)

##### Saving Throw against Spells (Spell)

#### Class Features

Your Class Features tells you what kind of gameplay altering things your Class can do. Think of these Class Features as powers that only a member of that Class can do.

Some class features are granted as you level up, rather than at first level. Be sure to read what level you gain a feature on.
