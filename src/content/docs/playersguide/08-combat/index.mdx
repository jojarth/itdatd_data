Combat is chaotic, fast-paced, and dangerous. Characters are in constant danger while fighting. A stray dagger can be the end of the line for your character.

The following rules take the chaos of combat and break it down into a cycle of ***Rounds, Phases, and Turns***. Many combats also include tests of Morale.

- ***Rounds -*** A round is a set of 9 Phases. A round represents roughly 6 seconds of in-game time.
- ***Phases -*** There are 9 Phases in 1 round, and they take place in a cycle. Once all 9 phases are done, the round is finished and the next round starts at Phase 1. A Phase has no time representation, but rather determines roughly how events fold out in a round.
    - Note that ***most*** actions take place between phases 2 and 8. Phases 1 and 9 are often only accessed when a character is able to act sooner or later, and are sometimes referred to as System Phases.
- ***Turns -*** When a character Acts or Reacts in a phase, it is called taking a turn (or taking "their" turn). A character can take no more than one turn in a phase. All creatures who take a turn in a given phase can complete it before moving to the next phase, and their turns are ***Adjudicated at the same time***.

The cycle of rounds, phases, and turns continues until one side has overcome the other.

### Rounds
Tracking rounds is mostly important for understanding battlefield effects and time. Spell effects and class features are often tracked by round. A round is an abstraction of what happens in about 6 seconds; thus, 10 rounds equal 1 minute if you need to track time.

### Phases
The meat and potatoes of combat. Things that you wish to do in combat must take place in certain phases. There are 9 phases in a round.

All creatures who act in a given phase have their activities ***Adjudicated at the same time***. Everything that begins, continues, or resolves in that phase should be adjudicated before moving to the next phase.

Phases are always handled in order, from 1 to 9. Anything adjudicated in an earlier phase can affect a later phase. For example, if you kill a charging orc in Phase 2, it cannot act in Phase 3. However, if both you and the Orc fire your bows in Phase 2, both of you have the chance to hit and deal damage, even if you rolled first, and killed the orc with your damage roll.

Unless an activity states otherwise (see Phase Lock below), an activity with a listed phase can be performed in that phase or a later phase, but never in an earlier phase. For example, moving is listed as *Phase 3.* Thus, you cannot move in *Phase 1* or *Phase 2,* but you can move in *Phase 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8,* or *9.*

### No Phase Listed
Some features may simply say they require an *Action* or *Master Action* without listing a phase. When this is the case, that activity can be performed in any phase.

### Phase listed as “Weapon”
If an activity has its phase listed as *Weapon*, use the Speed Factor of the weapon used for the activity to determine its earliest phase. If you are engaging in two-weapon fighting, use the Speed Factor of the weapon you are attacking with.

### Spells
Spells have the number of actions required, as well as the phase you can perform them on, listed in the "casting" section of each spell.

### Phase Lock
If an activity is Phase Locked, you must begin it in the listed phase. You cannot perform it earlier or delay it to a later phase.

An ability listed as *Phase 3* can be used in *Phase 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8,* or *9.* It simply cannot be used in *Phase 1* or *2.*

However, if an ability is listed as *Phase Lock 3,* it must be used in Phase 3. All channeled activities are Phase Locked.

## Adjudicating at the Same Time
Adjudicating at the same time means that all activities in a phase happen at the same time, regardless of who rolls first. If you are attempting to act in a phase, you get to do so unless a ***Reaction*** stops you.

**For example:** A goblin controlled by the GM and an archer controlled by a player fire arrows at each other during *Phase 2*. The player rolls first. The archer hits the goblin and deals enough damage to kill it. However, because the goblin is also acting in *Phase 2*, it still gets to make its attack despite the archer killing it "first."

The goblin then rolls its attack and damage, hits the archer, and deals 3 damage. At the end of *Phase 2*, the goblin has been slain by an arrow and the archer has taken 3 damage from the goblin's missile fire.

Things that happen in an earlier phase can affect a later phase. In the example above, the goblin gets to act in *Phase 2*, but its death prevents it from acting in *Phase 3* or later.

### Handling Movement
Even when playing on a grid, movement should be described in broad terms to your GM to get the sense of flow. For example: instead of picking up your figure, and moving them 4 squares (20 feet), you should instead say "I would like to move away from the Orcs", or "I would like to intercept the goblins".

As movement is all handled at the same time, the GM should take everything into consideration as movement is done. Thus, even though you may move your 4 squares to the west, since the goblins are moving slightly more north than west, your GM may help you by moving your figure 3 squares to the west and 1 square to the north.

Because of this open, and broad terms of movement, it is very possible that characters will move their characters, then shuffle them a bit as other creatures movements come to light. In the above example “I would like to intercept the goblins”, the player moves first and moves in a direct line 20 feet to the east. The GM moves the goblins south-west, instead of just west, which the player is no longer in line to intercept them. Since the player's intention was to intercept them, he should be allowed to “shuffle” where he moved to put his character in-line with his stated goal (so long as it stays within his initial move speed).

Effectively, when performing actions, intent of the action should be known, and players and GMs should work together in understanding the intent of the action.

## When do I act?
Your character can announce their intention to act during a phase at any time before the GM moves to the next phase. For example, if a stalwart warrior was planning on moving and fighting in melee, but a goblin archer fires an arrow at him, he can announce that he is going to throw his axe at the goblin during the same phase. Throwing the axe spends 1 Action and uses his *Master Action* for the round, but he may still spend his remaining Actions in later phases.

The warrior can throw the axe because it is already in his hand. He could not draw a bow and then fire it during that phase: those are two separate activities, and a character can Act only once per phase. An activity that explicitly includes drawing and firing would be an exception.

### Play in Good Faith
Because of the open nature of this combat system, it is very easy for a GM (or player) to “get revenge”, play with poor intention, or just simply be a menace. The easiest way to avoid this is announcing intent, keeping to the rules, and allowing grace and patience for all people playing.

***Because of this free-form combat, every table will have situations where good form and sportsmanship will be required. Remember that the GM isn't against you, and you aren't against the GM. It's supposed to be a collaborative story. Remember that you are here to have fun with your group, and that character set-backs (or even character death) can be fun.***

## Taking Your Turns

During each round, you have **3 Actions** and **1 Reaction** to use across the round's 9 phases. Unused Actions and Reactions are lost at the end of the round.

### Sluggish

**Sluggish (X)** delays when you can Act. Increase the earliest phase in which you can perform an activity by your Sluggish value. An activity with no phase listed has a base phase of 1 for this purpose, while an activity with a phase listed as *Weapon* uses the weapon's Speed Factor as its base phase.

For example, if you are Sluggish (2), an activity normally available in Phase 3 becomes available in Phase 5. A Phase-Locked activity has its locked phase increased by the same amount and remains Phase Locked. If Sluggish would delay an activity beyond Phase 9, you cannot perform that activity during the current round.

Sluggish does not delay Reactions unless a rule explicitly says otherwise. If multiple effects make you Sluggish, use only the highest Sluggish value.

### Slow

**Slow (X)** reduces the number of Actions you have each round by its Slow value, to a minimum of 0 Actions. For example, if you are Slow (1), you have 2 Actions each round instead of 3.

Slow does not reduce your number of Reactions or change how many Master Actions you can perform. If multiple effects make you Slow, use only the highest Slow value.

In any one phase, you can do one of the following:

- Act once, spending the Action cost of one activity.
- React once, if its trigger occurs and you have not already used your Reaction this round.
- Do nothing.

You cannot both Act and React in the same phase, nor can you Act or React more than once in that phase. This limit applies regardless of how many Actions an activity costs or how many movements, attacks, or other effects it includes.

## Actions

An **activity** is something you attempt to do, while **Actions** are the resource you spend to do it. Performing an activity is called **Acting**. Unless stated otherwise, an activity costs 1 Action.

### Taking Action

When you Act, choose one activity and spend its entire Action cost. You cannot begin an activity unless you have enough Actions remaining to pay its cost.

An activity that costs 2 or 3 Actions is still only one activity. Unless it is channeled, you pay the full cost and resolve the entire activity during the phase in which you Act. Paying more than 1 Action does not allow you to Act more than once during that phase.

An activity that costs 0 Actions, if a feature grants one, still counts as Acting and uses your turn for that phase.

Unless otherwise noted, you can attempt a specific activity only once per round. Spending Actions does not prevent you from taking later turns; if you have Actions remaining, you can Act again in a later phase.

### Action List

In the next few pages is a list of common actions you can perform in combat. They are listed out in “Action Cards”, which simply show how to adjudicate the action. This list is not exhaustive, and you can attempt anything in combat. When possible, use the provided rules to adjudicate certain actions. However, if an action you wish to perform isn’t on this list, work with your GM to find out how you can do it.

GMs, you can use the Action List to help you understand and determine how to adjudicate actions your players might perform.

### Phase List

There is no set phase list for actions and master actions. Because of this, a GM may have a hard time adjudicating which phase you can do something in. Below is a common list of Phases that actions might take place in, to allow you to better come up with ideas:

**Phases 1 - 3:** Although Phase 1 is usually the realm of system mechanics, or characters enchanted to be quicker, these 3 phases are usually reserved for volley combat and movement.

**Phases 4 - 6:** Many activities take place during these phases, and the most common are movement, melee attacks with lower Speed Factor weapons, and some spells. Many channeled spells start in these Phases, and finish in later phases.

**Phases 7 - 9:** Although Phase 9 is usually the realm of characters who are debilitated in some way, these three phases are usually reserved for slower, heavy weapon attacks, finishing channeled spells, some combat maneuvers, and even some slower spells.

## Master Actions

Master Actions are usually the most impactful activities you can perform in a round. Attacking with a weapon or casting a spell are almost always Master Actions.

You can perform only **one Master Action per round**. A Master Action still costs the listed number of Actions. Performing one does not prevent you from spending your remaining Actions in later phases, and it does not use your Reaction.

If a Master Action is interrupted, fails, or otherwise has no effect after you attempt it, it still counts as your Master Action for the round.

### Channeling

Every channeled activity is a **Phase-Locked Master Action**. When you begin a channel, spend its entire Action cost and use your Master Action for the round. Unlike an ordinary multi-Action activity, a channel does not resolve immediately. It resolves after a number of phases equal to its Action cost.

For example, ***Cost: 3 Actions; Master Action; Phase Lock 3; Channel*** begins in Phase 3 and resolves in Phase 6:

| Phase 3 | Phase 4 | Phase 5 | Phase 6 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Begin channel | Channeling | Channeling | Resolve effect |

Beginning a channel counts as Acting during its starting phase. Its later completion is automatic: it does not cost another Action and is not another turn. Channeling does not otherwise prevent you from spending any remaining Actions or using your Reaction in later phases.

While channeling, you are susceptible to interruption. If you take any damage, whether to Grit or Hit Points, or are required to make a Saving Throw after the starting phase and before the completion phase, the channel is interrupted. You lose the activity, all Actions spent on it remain spent, and you lose any resource used to begin it, such as a spell slot.

Unless an activity states otherwise, a channel must be able to finish before the end of the round. Because every channel is Phase Locked, its locked phase must leave enough phases for the channel to resolve.

### Uninterruptible Channel

An uninterruptible channel is written out as ***Uninterruptible Channel*** rather than marked with a single-letter modifier. For example: ***Cost: 3 Actions; Master Action; Phase Lock 3; Uninterruptible Channel***.

An Uninterruptible Channel is not interrupted by taking damage or being required to make a Saving Throw. It continues through its completion phase. This does not guarantee that the activity will have an effect: its target must still be valid and in range when it resolves, and any rule that specifically ends an Uninterruptible Channel can still do so.

## Reactions

You can take **one Reaction per round** when its trigger occurs. A Reaction does not spend one of your 3 Actions unless it says otherwise, but it counts as your turn for the phase. You cannot React in a phase in which you have already Acted, and after you React, you cannot Act later in that same phase. The most common Reactions are the Opportunity Attack and Shield Block.

Reactions are instant responses to their triggers and interrupt the flow of combat. Thus, a Reaction can affect its triggering activity, unlike other actions. For example, if a creature is moving out of an ally's threatened range and that ally's Opportunity Attack deals enough damage to kill it, the creature dies in the space it was leaving and does not finish its movement.

Using a Master Action does not prevent you from using your Reaction later in the round, provided you have not already used it and its trigger occurs in a later phase.

### Shield Block

**Trigger:** You are hit by an attack you can perceive while wielding a shield with which you are proficient.

After the attack's damage is rolled but before it is applied, use your Reaction to reduce that damage by your shield's **Block Value + your Combat Proficiency**, to a minimum of 0. Shield Block can be used against a melee, ranged, or spell attack, but not against an effect that does not hit you with an attack. It reduces the damage of the triggering hit only, including the increased damage of a critical hit.

After reducing the damage, make the shield's **Break Roll**, even if the damage was reduced to 0. Roll its Break Die. If the result is equal to or below its Break Chance, its condition worsens by one step: Good becomes Damaged, Damaged becomes Broken, and Broken becomes Destroyed. A Break Roll cannot worsen a shield by more than one step unless an effect explicitly says otherwise.

Effects can modify a shield's Break Chance. If its final Break Chance is 0 or lower, roll the Break Die twice; its condition worsens only if both dice roll a 1.

Shield statistics, condition effects, materials, and repair rules appear in Chapter 6: Equipment.

#### Shield Block Example

A character with Combat Proficiency 2 uses a shield with Block Value 3 and is hit for 9 damage. They use Shield Block and reduce the damage by 5, taking 4 damage. They then roll the shield's Break Die and compare the result to its current Break Chance.

### Master Reactions

A Master Reaction uses your Reaction and counts as your one Master Action for the round. You cannot use a Master Reaction if you have already used a Master Action that round, and using a Master Reaction prevents you from using a Master Action later in the round. It does not spend any of your 3 Actions or prevent you from spending them in later phases unless its rules say otherwise.

## Combat Supremacy

Combat Supremacy represents a tactical advantage one creature holds over another when the disadvantaged creature cannot devote its full attention to defending against that attacker.

Combat Supremacy is not a Condition. Instead, Conditions, Actions, positioning, and other effects may state that one creature **holds Combat Supremacy** against another. A Condition that causes foes to hold Combat Supremacy remains a Condition even though Combat Supremacy itself is not one.

Combat Supremacy is relative between individual creatures. Holding Combat Supremacy against one creature does not cause you to hold it against any other creature. Two creatures can even hold Combat Supremacy against one another simultaneously.

When you hold Combat Supremacy against a creature:

- You gain a +1 situation bonus to attack rolls against that creature.
- That creature suffers a -1 situation penalty to Reflex saving throws against effects you create.

Multiple sources of Combat Supremacy do not increase these bonuses or penalties. The effect that allows you to hold Combat Supremacy determines when it ends. If its requirements are no longer satisfied, you immediately cease holding Combat Supremacy.

## Combat Maneuvers

A Combat Maneuver is a Master Action used to Grapple, Disarm, or Trip a creature within your reach. Combat Maneuvers deal no damage. You normally need at least one free hand to attempt one, although certain Weapon Features allow a maneuver to be performed with a weapon.

Combat Maneuvers are resolved as Opposed Attribute Tests rather than attacks against Armor Class. Apply modifiers to the tested Attribute Scores before either participant rolls.

### Grapple

The attacker tests Brawn. The defender tests either Brawn or Agility. If the attacker wins, the defender becomes Grappled.

### Disarm

The attacker tests either Brawn or Precision. The defender tests either Brawn or Precision. If the attacker wins, the defender drops one held object selected by the attacker.

### Trip

The attacker tests either Brawn or Precision. The defender tests either Brawn or Agility. If the attacker wins, the defender falls Prone.

### Common Combat Maneuver Modifiers

| Circumstance | Modifier |
| --- | ---: |
| Each Size category larger than the opposing creature | +2 to the tested Attribute |
| Each Size category smaller than the opposing creature | -2 to the tested Attribute |
| Defending against Disarm while holding the selected object in two hands | +2 to the tested Attribute |
| Defending against Trip with four or more grounded legs | +2 to the tested Attribute |

The GM may apply other modifiers for unstable ground, unusual anatomy, restraints, leverage, or similarly important circumstances. If a creature cannot meaningfully resist, the maneuver succeeds without a roll. If the maneuver is physically impossible, it fails without a roll.

Save vs Binding remains appropriate for magical bindings, animated restraints, environmental entanglements, and other effects that do not involve an active contest between creatures.

## Defining Turn Based Effects

Many activities, features, and spells indicate that their effects last for a certain amount of time, often measured in rounds.

- When any effect lasts for a time frame measured in rounds, note that Phase that the effect took place on. The effect ends at the end of that Phase after the number of rounds pass.
- If a spell's duration is measured in time units, instead of tracking by the phase, you track it to the end of the round where the spell would have ended. Essentially, if an effect's duration is measured in time rather than rounds, what phase it ends on isn't as important as knowing roughly when the spell will end.
- If an effect specifies "for the remainder of the round", then the effects end at the end of the current round, regardless of what phase the effect takes place.
